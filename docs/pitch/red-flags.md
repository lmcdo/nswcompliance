# Red flags — the four engagement shapes to walk away from

**Rule.** If a first email carries any of these shapes, do not quote and do not schedule. Reply once with a decline and close the thread. Every one of these engagements ends badly and the decision to accept it is made in the first email, not later.

Four flags, in order of how often they appear.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Red flag 1 — "We need a signature by \[date\]"

**What the email says.** Any variant of:

- *"We're closing a funding round on the 30th and need an audit letter for the data room."*
- *"Our board meeting is next Tuesday, can you turn this around by then?"*
- *"Our insurance renewal needs a third-party attestation this week."*
- *"We just need something to show \[the acquirer / the regulator / the investor\], nothing formal."*

**Why it's a walk.** The deliverable they want isn't the audit — it's the attestation, and they picked you because you're cheap and available. The first specific finding will be the one that delays the signature, and every subsequent conversation will be about softening it. The finding gets buried either way. You end up with a bad reference from a buyer who blames you for the signature not arriving on time.

**What to reply.** *"I don't ship audits on external deadlines — the report lands when the evidence lands. For a data-room artefact you'd be better served by a firm that does formal attestations; happy to name a couple if useful."*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Red flag 2 — "Can you sign an NDA before we tell you what it's about"

**What the email says.** Any variant of:

- *"We can't share details without an NDA — can you sign the attached before we jump on a call?"*
- *"The product is under embargo, need mutual NDA first."*
- *"Our legal requires an MSA + NDA + IP assignment before scoping."*

**Why it's a walk.** Two failure modes. First, if the product needs an NDA before you can even find out what it does, the qualifying diagnostic can't run — and every engagement that skipped the diagnostic on my own project turned into rework. Second, an MSA before scope is a paperwork organisation and the audit will be redlined into unrecognisability by their legal team; you'll spend two weeks on documents and get paid for one.

**What to reply.** *"Happy to sign an NDA when we've agreed there's a fit — that's what the 30-minute qualifying diagnostic is for. If public-surface probes and three questions can't happen without paperwork first, I'm not the right fit; we'd both spend more on the setup than the work."*

**One exception, deliberate.** A named person at a company you already trust, forwarding a mutual NDA and a one-line description. In that case sign, then schedule the diagnostic. Everything else, decline.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Red flag 3 — "Can you also do X while you're in there"

**What the email says.** Anywhere in the scoping thread:

- *"Can you also review our AWS setup?"*
- *"While you're looking, could you sanity-check the ML model performance?"*
- *"We'd love your take on our roadmap — informal, no deliverable needed."*
- *"Can this include some security review as well? Our SOC2 auditor asked."*

**Why it's a walk.** The scope expands from "one thing I can price" to "everything Lawrence is looking at while we have him." You will do all of it, because none of it costs you individually. You will be paid for one. And the "informal, no deliverable" ones are the highest-risk — a verbal opinion you can't stand behind because you didn't work it up properly is the opinion the buyer will quote back to their board.

**What to reply.** *"The audit is scoped to what's in the SOW; anything else is a separate engagement. Happy to price the additional item after this one lands — that keeps both scopes honest."*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Red flag 4 — "The last person we hired for this missed X"

**What the email says.** Any variant of:

- *"Our previous auditor didn't catch \[obvious thing\], can you make sure you look at that specifically?"*
- *"We had a consultant last year but the report was too soft — we need someone who'll actually push."*
- *"We want an auditor who'll challenge our engineers."*

**Why it's a walk.** The buyer is pre-scripting the finding. Sometimes the previous auditor was bad. More often the previous auditor was fine and the buyer wanted a finding the evidence didn't support — and now they're shopping for someone who will produce it. The moment you fail to reproduce their preferred finding, you become the second bad auditor.

**What to reply.** *"I only report what I can prove by planting the defect and watching a check go red. If \[thing\] is really there, the census will find it; if it isn't, the report will say so. If a specific finding is the success criterion, I'm the wrong hire — you want a consultant, not an auditor."*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What to do with a maybe

If an email has none of the four but you're still uneasy, run the qualifying diagnostic (`qualifying-diagnostic.docx`, in this same pack) and let the three questions decide. The point of the diagnostic is that the decision to walk stops being personal — the questions either land or they don't.

If the buyer resists the diagnostic itself — "we don't have time for that, can we just start" — that's actually red flag 1 wearing a different jacket. Same reply.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## How to hold this line

Once you decline, do not follow up. Do not send a *"but if things change…"* email. The buyer knows how to find you. Chasing a buyer you've already declined is how the decline gets un-declined at 20% of your quoted rate.

Every audit you don't take is one more week of runway for the ones you do.
